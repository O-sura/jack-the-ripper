Robots, and the things you cannot un-publish

7 February 2024

A thing I learned the hard way: a search engine will cheerfully index anything it can reach, including the half-finished rubbish you forgot was still reachable.

Last year someone emailed me quoting a sentence I had definitely never published. It was from a post I abandoned months earlier and left sitting on the server. Google had found it, indexed it, and served it to a total stranger.

The standard fix is a file called robots.txt. It lives at the root of a site — stick /robots.txt on the end of any domain and you can read it, mine included. It is a plain text list of paths you are asking crawlers to leave alone:

User-agent: *
Disallow: /some-folder/

That is the entire format. Every site you have ever used has one. Go and look at a few — it is one of those things nobody tells you exists, and then you start seeing it everywhere.

Here is the part that took me an embarrassingly long time to internalise. robots.txt is a request, not a lock. It politely asks well-behaved crawlers not to index a path. It does absolutely nothing to stop a human being from opening the file, reading exactly which folders you would rather they did not visit, and typing one of them straight into the address bar.

Which, now that I write it down, means a robots.txt is a tidy little list of everything you are most embarrassed about, published at an entirely predictable URL, for anyone who thinks to look.

If something genuinely must not be seen, it needs a password. Not a polite note.

Anyway. Mine is up, the crawlers have backed off, and the half-finished posts have stopped turning up in search results.
